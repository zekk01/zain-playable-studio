Pit Protocol UNREAL ENGINE 5 · META QUEST

The headset is a phone with lenses. The client wanted a Formula 1 pit lane. The job was making both of those true at the same time.

Engine
Unreal Engine 5, C++ and Blueprint
Runs on
Meta Quest headsets, six synchronized stations, one PC host
Where
Yas Marina / Abu Dhabi GP weekend activations for Emaar and Address Hospitality; Address Beach Resort, December 2025
Timeline
Five months to a demo-ready build
My role
Head of game design, and the engineer on the core: streaming, optimization, cinematics, crew AI
Result
Six stations in sync under 20 ms, a 30 fps floor on the headset, 98% uptime across event days

The problem

Everything the brief asked for is expensive on a mobile chip: a full F1 car, a garage with a crowd behind the glass, a pit crew that moves like a pit crew, six players at once, and a live leaderboard the crowd can read from the bar. Standalone Quest content is normally built small to fit. That would have looked like a mobile game at a Grand Prix.

Stream assets, not frames

There were three ways to go, and the obvious two were both wrong for a live venue.

CHOSE
The PC keeps the content and the clock; each headset receives the level ahead of time and renders it locally.
OVER
Cutting the content down to what a Quest can hold, or rendering on the PC and streaming video to every headset.
BECAUSE
Video streaming puts every frame through venue wifi, the least reliable thing in the building, and adds latency you feel in your stomach. Cutting content loses the thing the activation was paid for.

Asset streaming moves the heavy traffic to the gaps between sessions. During a run, the only thing on the network is state: the clock, positions, who has done what. That is a few bytes, and it is why six stations could stay within 20 ms of each other on a network I did not control. Fig 1.1

Preload, then play

The level is fully resident before a group puts on headsets. The PC pushes the content set to each station, each station warms its caches, and the host confirms all six are ready before the clock can start. Nothing loads mid-run, so nothing hitches mid-run. It is also why the stations could be brought up in the morning and stay up all day: the expensive path runs once, in a window nobody is watching.

Holding the floor

The frame budget at 30 fps is 33.3 milliseconds, and a headset that misses it makes people feel sick, which is a worse failure than a crash. I set 30 as the floor, not the target: the typical frame ran well under budget, and the headroom is what kept the floor a floor when six headsets were warm and a station had been running for hours. The optimization pass was the mobile-VR discipline done in the order that pays: lighting baked, LODs tuned by where players actually stand, draw calls batched, a texture budget enforced, particle counts capped, and the crew animations kept on a fixed cost. Fig 1.2

Cinematics that are actually there

The briefing and the pit-stop moment could have been a video on a screen in the world. I staged them in-engine instead: the camera moves on rails, the crew animations are timed to the beat of the car stopping, and the player keeps full head tracking throughout. A video is a flat rectangle in a room that is otherwise real; the moment you turn your head it stops being a pit lane. Timing the crew to the car’s stop also means the sequence adapts when a group is slow to settle. The sync point is an event, not a timestamp. Fig 1.3

Crew that works around a human

The AI crew changes the wheels the player is not changing. The hard part is not the wheel. It is that a real person is standing in the box with their arms out, and they never stand where you planned. Each crew member owns a slot (a wheel, the jack, the gun) with an approach path, a work volume, and an exit. The player’s station is a reserved zone the crew treats as solid. If a path crosses it, the crew member waits rather than pushes through, and the sequence resumes on its next beat. Crew never collide with the player, never collide with each other, and never break the timing of the stop. Fig 1.4

SKETCH · C++ · CREW MEMBERreduced to the rule that matters
// One crew member, one job. The rule: do the job, never through the player.
void ACrewMember::Advance(float Dt)
{
    switch (Step)
    {
    case EStep::Approach:
        if (PlayerZone.Overlaps(NextWaypoint())) { Wait(Dt); break; }   // yield; resume on the next beat
        MoveAlong(Path, Dt);
        if (AtSlot()) Step = EStep::GunOff;
        break;
    case EStep::GunOff:   PlayTimed(Anim.GunOff,   [&]{ Step = EStep::WheelOff; }); break;
    case EStep::WheelOff: PlayTimed(Anim.WheelOff, [&]{ Step = EStep::WheelOn;  }); break;
    case EStep::WheelOn:  PlayTimed(Anim.WheelOn,  [&]{ Step = EStep::GunOn;    }); break;
    case EStep::GunOn:    PlayTimed(Anim.GunOn,    [&]{ Step = EStep::Clear;    }); break;
    case EStep::Clear:    MoveAlong(ExitPath, Dt);                                 break;
    }
}
// Crew never collide with the player: the zone is solid to them. Crew never collide with each other:
// each slot owns its work volume, and approach paths are planned around the volumes already claimed.

Unseen Blade UNITY · ANDROID

You are blind, and sound is the map. That is one rule, so it had to be one shader.

Engine
Unity, C# and HLSL
Runs on
Android, Google Play; first playable April 2026
Content
Three campaign chapters, story mode, shadow trials, training grounds
Studio
City Crafters
My role
Concept and design, the echo-location rendering, the stealth and detection AI, combat
Result
Shipped. A stealth game you can read without seeing it

The rule

The world is dark. Every sound sends a wave outward from where it happened, and surfaces light up where the wave passes, then fade. Your own footsteps show you the room. An enemy’s footsteps show you the enemy. A thrown tile shows you the corridor behind them. And every one of those sounds is also something the enemies hear.

One shader for every object

The obvious way to build this is per object: a script on each mesh that reacts to sounds. It does not scale, and it looks wrong at the seams where two objects meet. I wrote the echo as a single material that works on any mesh, because it needs only one thing every mesh already has: the world-space position of each pixel. For each active sound the shader knows the origin and the start time. The wavefront’s radius is speed times elapsed time. A pixel lights up when its distance from the origin is within the wave’s thickness of that radius, and the light fades as the wave spreads. No per-object setup, no UVs, no baked data. Drop the material on a wall, a blade, a guard, a floor tile, and it obeys. Fig 2.1

SKETCH · HLSL · ECHO MATERIALfragment stage, reduced to the idea
// _Pulse[i] = (origin.xyz, startTime). _Speed, _Width and _Range are in world units.
float echo = 0;
for (int i = 0; i < _PulseCount; i++)
{
    float3 o    = _Pulse[i].xyz;
    float  r    = (_Time.y - _Pulse[i].w) * _Speed;      // how far this wavefront has travelled
    float  d    = distance(IN.worldPos, o);              // how far this pixel is from the sound
    float  ring = 1 - saturate(abs(d - r) / _Width);     // 1 on the wavefront, 0 away from it
    echo += ring * saturate(1 - r / _Range);              // fades as the pulse spreads
}
return float4(_EchoColor.rgb * saturate(echo), 1);
// Nothing here depends on the mesh: no UVs, no vertex colours, no per-object setup. Any surface obeys.

One event, two listeners

Sounds go through a small bus. The renderer reads it to fill the shader’s pulse array (a handful of active pulses is plenty; old ones expire). The AI reads the same bus to decide what the guards heard: loudness times distance falloff against a hearing threshold. Because both systems consume the same record, the game is honest by construction. If you saw the room light up, the guard heard it too. There is no separate noise stat to keep in sync with what the player sees. Fig 2.2

The tension that makes it a game

To see, you must make sound. To make sound is to be found. Sneaking is quiet, so it shows you less; sprinting shows you everything and everyone. Combat is loud on purpose, so a fight anywhere is a fight everyone comes to. The guard AI runs a short awareness ladder driven by what it heard and how recently, with timeouts that bring it back down, so a careful player can make one mistake and recover from it. Fig 2.3

Target Destroyed UNITY · ANDROID

Drone interceptions were the news in the UAE. Two weeks later the game was on Google Play. Speed is a design constraint like any other.

Engine
Unity, C#
Runs on
Android, Google Play; portrait and landscape; 60 fps on modern phones
Timeline
Two weeks from idea to store submission
Meta
Hangar upgrades, pilot ranks, daily challenges, three global leaderboards
My role
The whole build: loop, enemy structure, HUD, economy, leaderboards
Studio
City Crafters

The constraint

The idea only worked if it shipped while people were still talking about drones. That gave me fourteen days and a rule: anything that is not the loop or a reason to come back gets cut. No cutscenes, no story, no menu deeper than two taps. The first week bought the loop; the second bought the reasons to come back. Fig 3.1

One clock is the whole economy

A sortie is thirty seconds. Every kill adds time; nothing else does. Gold high-value targets add three times as much; combos multiply. That one mechanic gives you difficulty, pacing and scoring from three numbers: time per kill, spawn rate, and the gold multiplier. Tuning the game was tuning those, watching sessions, and tuning again. It also means the game ends by itself, which is exactly what a session on a phone needs. Fig 3.2

SKETCH · C# · THE CLOCKreduced to the rule that matters
// The whole game is one clock. Kills buy time; nothing else does.
void OnKill(Target t)
{
    float bonus = t.IsGold ? baseBonus * 3f : baseBonus;      // gold targets: three times the time
    clock  = Mathf.Min(clock + bonus * ComboMultiplier(), clockCap);
    combo  = Mathf.Min(combo + 1, comboMax);
    score += t.Value * ComboMultiplier();
    hud.Flash(t);                                                // loud feedback: a kill has to feel like time
}

void Update()
{
    clock -= Time.deltaTime;                                     // one second per second, no mercy
    if (clock <= 0f) EndSortie();                                 // the game ends by itself
}

Enemy structure and the HUD

Targets are a small set of behaviours (straight runners, weavers, high-value gold) spawned by a director that keeps the screen busy but readable. The HUD is a FLIR-style military display: smart lock-on picks the target you meant, missile lock reads at a glance, and the feedback for a kill is loud. Portrait and landscape both work because the loop is aim-and-fire; the layout adapts, the rules do not.

Reasons to come back

Kills earn Intel Points; Intel Points buy hangar upgrades; upgrades move you up the pilot ranks. Daily challenges reset the goal every morning. Three global leaderboards, held on the server, so a thirty-second sortie can still put you on a board. Fig 3.3

Lala Poker UNITY · C++ · iOS, ANDROID, WEB

Six games, one engine. The client draws the cards. It never decides them.

Engine
Unity clients on iOS, Android and WebGL; the game core in C++
Games
Texas Hold’em, Omaha, multi-table tournaments, Blackjack, Roulette, lottery
Period
2023 to 2025, live throughout
Studio
Lala Gaming
My role
The game logic, the security model, and the payout and balance math
Result
4.6 on the App Store; live on three platforms

Authority

Games with money-shaped stakes have one rule above all: the client is not trusted. Every table runs in a C++ core that owns the deck, the shuffle, the turn order, the pot and the payouts. Clients send intents (call, raise, fold, hit, spin), the core validates each one against the state machine, and every player receives only the view they are entitled to. Your hole cards exist on the server; your opponent’s never reach your device in any form. Fig 4.1

SKETCH · C++ · TABLE ENGINEreduced to the rule that matters
// Every client message is an intent. The table decides whether it is legal. Nothing else does.
Result Table::Apply(PlayerId who, const Action& a)
{
    if (who != seats[turn].id)                    return Reject("not your turn");
    if (!a.IsLegalFor(street, minRaise, toCall))  return Reject("illegal action");
    if (a.amount > seats[turn].stack)             return Reject("insufficient chips");

    ApplyToPot(who, a);            // side pots fall out of the same step when someone is all-in short
    AdvanceTurn();                 // may close the street, deal the next card, or trigger showdown
    return Commit(ViewFor(who));  // each seat gets its own view: only what that player may see
}
// The deck lives here, shuffled from a cryptographic source. Hole cards never leave the core except
// to their owner, and every hand is replayable from its log, so any dispute has exactly one answer.

One state machine per game, one shape for all of them

Poker is a sequence of streets, each of which is a loop of act, validate, next seat, until every live player has matched or one is left. Side pots fall out of the same loop when someone goes all-in short. Omaha changes the hand evaluator, not the loop. Tournaments wrap tables in blind levels and rebalance seats between hands. Blackjack and Roulette are shorter machines with the same discipline: intent in, validation, resolution, view out. Fig 4.2

Where the edge lives

I designed the balance model with one principle: the random number generator is never tuned; the payout table is. Poker’s fairness is a uniform shuffle from a cryptographic source; the house earns rake. In house games the edge is in arithmetic anyone can check: a wheel with more pockets than the payout implies, a dealer who acts last, a lottery prize pool that is a fixed share of ticket sales. Jackpots grow from a fixed contribution per pot, so the pool can always pay what it advertises. I verified the return-to-player targets by simulation before anything went live, and the economy stayed solvent as chips, jackpots and VIP tiers were layered on top. Fig 4.3

Security beyond the table

Sessions are authenticated, every action is validated server-side against the current state, hidden state never leaves the core, and resolution is deterministic, so any hand can be replayed from its log to settle a dispute with one answer.

Holographic pipeline UNITY · C++ · SWAVE HXR

A hologram can’t show a surface. It shows points of light. So I turned surfaces into clouds of points, on purpose.

Engine
Unity (C#) with a C++ bridge to Swave’s holographic engine
Hardware
Swave HXR, a spatial light modulator with sub-300 nm pixels: the first real-time true holographic displays
Timeline
September to December 2023
Company
Swave Photonics
My role
Sole engineer on the Unity side: the slicing and point-cloud algorithm, and the streaming bridge
Result
Live 3D content from a game engine on a display that had never shown any

Why points

A holographic display does not rasterize triangles. It reconstructs a light field, and the natural input for that computation is a set of point emitters in space, each contributing a wave. Feed it a mesh and you have nothing. Feed it the right points and you get an object hanging in the air. The job was turning what Unity has, shaded surfaces, into what the display wants, well-distributed points, fast enough to be live. Fig 5.1

Slice, keep the shell, sample

The algorithm slices any mesh with a stack of planes, keeps only the outer shell of each slice, and discards the interior. Light inside an object is wasted computation and, worse, visible noise. Points are then sampled on the shell with density proportional to area, so a big flat face and a small detailed one both look solid. Fig 5.2

Randomize on purpose

The samples are jittered. A regular sampling grid lines up with the display’s own pixel structure and produces moiré and ghost patterns in the reconstruction. Randomized positions have no repeating structure to interfere with, and re-randomizing per frame turns any residual artifact into a soft shimmer instead of a fixed pattern. This was the difference between a demo and an object. Fig 5.3

The bridge

The point stream leaves Unity through a C++ pipeline into Swave’s hologram engine, which computes the interference pattern and drives the HXR hardware. Meshes and shaders on the Unity side were optimized for that budget: fewer, better points beat more.

SKETCH · C# · SHELL TO POINTSreduced to the rule that matters
// Shell → points. Area-weighted so big faces stay solid; jittered so no two frames line up.
foreach (var tri in shell.Triangles)
{
    int n = Mathf.CeilToInt(tri.Area * pointsPerUnitArea);
    for (int k = 0; k < n; k++)
    {
        float u = Random.value, v = Random.value;               // uniform on the triangle
        if (u + v > 1f) { u = 1f - u; v = 1f - v; }
        Vector3 p = tri.A + u * (tri.B - tri.A) + v * (tri.C - tri.A);
        p += tri.Normal * Random.Range(-jitter, jitter);           // break the grid, on purpose
        stream.Write(p, tri.Colour);                               // off to the C++ bridge, then the hologram engine
    }
}

The loop

A lesson is a short drill: tap the matching pairs, say the word, pick the right image. Drills pay in coins. Coins build the city. New districts open new vocabulary, which means new drills. The whole game is that loop, gated so you cannot buy your way past words you have not learned. Fig 6.1

An economy that teaches

The design problem with a learning game is that the fun part (building) and the useful part (drilling) pull in opposite directions. The economy is the bridge. Lessons are the only reliable source of coins, buildings are the sink, and the gate to each new district checks level, wallet and mastery of the current words. Streaks and challenges add a little income so a bad day does not stall the city, but they never replace lessons. Fig 6.2

SKETCH · C# · THE GATEreduced to the rule that matters
// A district opens when the city has earned it: level, wallet, and mastery of the current words.
bool CanUnlock(District d) =>
    city.Level      >= d.MinLevel
 && wallet.Coins    >= d.Cost
 && lessons.Mastery(d.Vocabulary) >= d.MasteryToOpen;   // coins alone never open a door

// The drill selector: words you miss come back sooner. Mastery is what the gate reads.
Word NextDrill(Vocabulary v) =>
    v.Words.OrderByDescending(w => w.Misses * 2 - w.Hits + w.DaysSinceSeen).First();

AI and shaders, solo

The city’s inhabitants go about their day on their own paths and schedules, so the place feels alive between lessons, and the drill selector weighs the words you have been missing so repetition lands where it is needed. The look is custom shaders: the build-up when a new building rises, and a stylized city that reads on a small screen. Every system in the beta, from content spawning to audio, came from the same keyboard. Fig 6.3
