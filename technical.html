Pit Protocol UNREAL ENGINE 5 · META QUEST

The headset is a phone with lenses. The client wanted a Formula 1 pit lane. The job was making both of those true at the same time.

Engine
Unreal Engine 5, C++ and Blueprint
Runs on
Meta Quest headsets, six synchronized stations, one PC host
Where
Yas Marina / Abu Dhabi GP weekend activations for Emaar and Address Hospitality; Address Beach Resort, December 2025
Timeline
Five months to a demo-ready build
My role
Head of game design, and the engineer on the core: streaming, optimization, cinematics, crew AI
Result
Six stations in sync under 20 ms, a 30 fps floor on the headset, 98% uptime across event days

The problem

Everything the brief asked for is expensive on a mobile chip: a full F1 car, a garage with a crowd behind the glass, a pit crew that moves like a pit crew, six players at once, and a live leaderboard the crowd can read from the bar. Standalone Quest content is normally built small to fit. That would have looked like a mobile game at a Grand Prix.

Stream assets, not frames

There were three ways to go, and the obvious two were both wrong for a live venue.

CHOSE
The PC keeps the content and the clock; each headset receives the level ahead of time and renders it locally.
OVER
Cutting the content down to what a Quest can hold, or rendering on the PC and streaming video to every headset.
BECAUSE
Video streaming puts every frame through venue wifi, the least reliable thing in the building, and adds latency you feel in your stomach. Cutting content loses the thing the activation was paid for.

Asset streaming moves the heavy traffic to the gaps between sessions. During a run, the only thing on the network is state: the clock, positions, who has done what. That is a few bytes, and it is why six stations could stay within 20 ms of each other on a network I did not control. Fig 1.1

Preload, then play

The level is fully resident before a group puts on headsets. The PC pushes the content set to each station, each station warms its caches, and the host confirms all six are ready before the clock can start. Nothing loads mid-run, so nothing hitches mid-run. It is also why the stations could be brought up in the morning and stay up all day: the expensive path runs once, in a window nobody is watching.

Holding the floor

The frame budget at 30 fps is 33.3 milliseconds, and a headset that misses it makes people feel sick, which is a worse failure than a crash. I set 30 as the floor, not the target: the typical frame ran well under budget, and the headroom is what kept the floor a floor when six headsets were warm and a station had been running for hours. The optimization pass was the mobile-VR discipline done in the order that pays: lighting baked, LODs tuned by where players actually stand, draw calls batched, a texture budget enforced, particle counts capped, and the crew animations kept on a fixed cost. Fig 1.2

Cinematics that are actually there

The briefing and the pit-stop moment could have been a video on a screen in the world. I staged them in-engine instead: the camera moves on rails, the crew animations are timed to the beat of the car stopping, and the player keeps full head tracking throughout. A video is a flat rectangle in a room that is otherwise real; the moment you turn your head it stops being a pit lane. Timing the crew to the car’s stop also means the sequence adapts when a group is slow to settle. The sync point is an event, not a timestamp. Fig 1.3

Crew that works around a human

The AI crew changes the wheels the player is not changing. The hard part is not the wheel. It is that a real person is standing in the box with their arms out, and they never stand where you planned. Each crew member owns a slot (a wheel, the jack, the gun) with an approach path, a work volume, and an exit. The player’s station is a reserved zone the crew treats as solid. If a path crosses it, the crew member waits rather than pushes through, and the sequence resumes on its next beat. Crew never collide with the player, never collide with each other, and never break the timing of the stop. Fig 1.4

SKETCH · C++ · CREW MEMBERreduced to the rule that matters
// One crew member, one job. The rule: do the job, never through the player.
void ACrewMember::Advance(float Dt)
{
    switch (Step)
    {
    case EStep::Approach:
        if (PlayerZone.Overlaps(NextWaypoint())) { Wait(Dt); break; }   // yield; resume on the next beat
        MoveAlong(Path, Dt);
        if (AtSlot()) Step = EStep::GunOff;
        break;
    case EStep::GunOff:   PlayTimed(Anim.GunOff,   [&]{ Step = EStep::WheelOff; }); break;
    case EStep::WheelOff: PlayTimed(Anim.WheelOff, [&]{ Step = EStep::WheelOn;  }); break;
    case EStep::WheelOn:  PlayTimed(Anim.WheelOn,  [&]{ Step = EStep::GunOn;    }); break;
    case EStep::GunOn:    PlayTimed(Anim.GunOn,    [&]{ Step = EStep::Clear;    }); break;
    case EStep::Clear:    MoveAlong(ExitPath, Dt);                                 break;
    }
}

Unseen Blade UNITY · ANDROID

You are blind, and sound is the map. That is one rule, so it had to be one shader.

Engine
Unity, C# and HLSL
Runs on
Android, Google Play; first playable April 2026
Content
Three campaign chapters, story mode, shadow trials, training grounds
Studio
City Crafters
My role
Concept and design, the echo-location rendering, the stealth and detection AI, combat
Result
Shipped. A stealth game you can read without seeing it

The rule

The world is dark. Every sound sends a wave outward from where it happened, and surfaces light up where the wave passes, then fade. Your own footsteps show you the room. An enemy’s footsteps show you the enemy. A thrown tile shows you the corridor behind them. And every one of those sounds is also something the enemies hear.

One shader for every object

The obvious way to build this is per object: a script on each mesh that reacts to sounds. It does not scale, and it looks wrong at the seams where two objects meet. I wrote the echo as a single material that works on any mesh, because it needs only one thing every mesh already has: the world-space position of each pixel. For each active sound the shader knows the origin and the start time. The wavefront’s radius is speed times elapsed time. A pixel lights up when its distance from the origin is within the wave’s thickness of that radius, and the light fades as the wave spreads. No per-object setup, no UVs, no baked data. Drop the material on a wall, a blade, a guard, a floor tile, and it obeys. Fig 2.1

SKETCH · HLSL · ECHO MATERIALfragment stage, reduced to the idea
// _Pulse[i] = (origin.xyz, startTime). _Speed, _Width and _Range are in world units.
float echo = 0;
for (int i = 0; i < _PulseCount; i++)
{
    float3 o    = _Pulse[i].xyz;
    float  r    = (_Time.y - _Pulse[i].w) * _Speed;      // how far this wavefront has travelled
    float  d    = distance(IN.worldPos, o);              // how far this pixel is from the sound
    float  ring = 1 - saturate(abs(d - r) / _Width);     // 1 on the wavefront, 0 away from it
    echo += ring * saturate(1 - r / _Range);              // fades as the pulse spreads
}
return float4(_EchoColor.rgb * saturate(echo), 1);
// Nothing here depends on the mesh: no UVs, no vertex colours, no per-object setup. Any surface obeys.

One event, two listeners

Sounds go through a small bus. The renderer reads it to fill the shader’s pulse array (a handful of active pulses is plenty; old ones expire). The AI reads the same bus to decide what the guards heard: loudness times distance falloff against a hearing threshold. Because both systems consume the same record, the game is honest by construction. If you saw the room light up, the guard heard it too. There is no separate noise stat to keep in sync with what the player sees. Fig 2.2

The tension that makes it a game

To see, you must make sound. To make sound is to be found. Sneaking is quiet, so it shows you less; sprinting shows you everything and everyone. Combat is loud on purpose, so a fight anywhere is a fight everyone comes to. The guard AI runs a short awareness ladder driven by what it heard and how recently, with timeouts that bring it back down, so a careful player can make one mistake and recover from it. Fig 2.3

Drone interceptions were the news in the UAE. Two weeks later the game was on Google Play. Speed is a design constraint like any other.

The constraint

The idea only worked if it shipped while people were still talking about drones. That gave me fourteen days and a rule: anything that is not the loop or a reason to come back gets cut. No cutscenes, no story, no menu deeper than two taps. The first week bought the loop; the second bought the reasons to come back. Fig 3.1

One clock is the whole economy

A sortie is thirty seconds. Every kill adds time; nothing else does. Gold high-value targets add three times as much; combos multiply. That one mechanic gives you difficulty, pacing and scoring from three numbers: time per kill, spawn rate, and the gold multiplier. Tuning the game was tuning those, watching sessions, and tuning again. It also means the game ends by itself, which is exactly what a session on a phone needs. Fig 3.2

SKETCH · C# · THE CLOCKreduced to the rule that matters
// The whole game is one clock. Kills buy time; nothing else does.
void OnKill(Target t)
{
    float bonus = t.IsGold ? baseBonus * 3f : baseBonus;      // gold targets: three times the time
    clock  = Mathf.Min(clock + bonus * ComboMultiplier(), clockCap);
    combo  = Mathf.Min(combo + 1, comboMax);
    score += t.Value * ComboMultiplier();
    hud.Flash(t);                                                // loud feedback: a kill has to feel like time
}

void Update()
{
    clock -= Time.deltaTime;                                     // one second per second, no mercy
    if (clock <= 0f) EndSortie();                                 // the game ends by itself
}

Enemy structure and the HUD

Targets are a small set of behaviours (straight runners, weavers, high-value gold) spawned by a director that keeps the screen busy but readable. The HUD is a FLIR-style military display: smart lock-on picks the target you meant, missile lock reads at a glance, and the feedback for a kill is loud. Portrait and landscape both work because the loop is aim-and-fire; the layout adapts, the rules do not.

Reasons to come back

Kills earn Intel Points; Intel Points buy hangar upgrades; upgrades move you up the pilot ranks. Daily challenges reset the goal every morning. Three global leaderboards, held on the server, so a thirty-second sortie can still put you on a board. Fig 3.3

Lala Poker UNITY · C++ · iOS, ANDROID, WEB

Six games, one engine. The client draws the cards. It never decides them.

Engine
Unity clients on iOS, Android and WebGL; the game core in C++
Games
Texas Hold’em, Omaha, multi-table tournaments, Blackjack, Roulette, lottery
Period
2023 to 2025, live throughout
Studio
Lala Gaming
My role
The game logic, the security model, and the payout and balance math
Result
4.6 on the App Store; live on three platforms

Authority

Games with money-shaped stakes have one rule above all: the client is not trusted. Every table runs in a C++ core that owns the deck, the shuffle, the turn order, the pot and the payouts. Clients send intents (call, raise, fold, hit, spin), the core validates each one against the state machine, and every player receives only the view they are entitled to. Your hole cards exist on the server; your opponent’s never reach your device in any form. Fig 4.1

SKETCH · C++ · TABLE ENGINEreduced to the rule that matters
// Every client message is an intent. The table decides whether it is legal. Nothing else does.
Result Table::Apply(PlayerId who, const Action& a)
{
    if (who != seats[turn].id)                    return Reject("not your turn");
    if (!a.IsLegalFor(street, minRaise, toCall))  return Reject("illegal action");
    if (a.amount > seats[turn].stack)             return Reject("insufficient chips");

    ApplyToPot(who, a);            // side pots fall out of the same step when someone is all-in short
    AdvanceTurn();                 // may close the street, deal the next card, or trigger showdown
    return Commit(ViewFor(who));  // each seat gets its own view: only what that player may see
}
// The deck lives here, shuffled from a cryptographic source. Hole cards never leave the core except
// to their owner, and every hand is replayable from its log, so any dispute has exactly one answer.

One state machine per game, one shape for all of them

Poker is a sequence of streets, each of which is a loop of act, validate, next seat, until every live player has matched or one is left. Side pots fall out of the same loop when someone goes all-in short. Omaha changes the hand evaluator, not the loop. Tournaments wrap tables in blind levels and rebalance seats between hands. Blackjack and Roulette are shorter machines with the same discipline: intent in, validation, resolution, view out. Fig 4.2

Where the edge lives

I designed the balance model with one principle: the random number generator is never tuned; the payout table is. Poker’s fairness is a uniform shuffle from a cryptographic source; the house earns rake. In house games the edge is in arithmetic anyone can check: a wheel with more pockets than the payout implies, a dealer who acts last, a lottery prize pool that is a fixed share of ticket sales. Jackpots grow from a fixed contribution per pot, so the pool can always pay what it advertises. I verified the return-to-player targets by simulation before anything went live, and the economy stayed solvent as chips, jackpots and VIP tiers were layered on top. Fig 4.3

Security beyond the table

Sessions are authenticated, every action is validated server-side against the current state, hidden state never leaves the core, and resolution is deterministic, so any hand can be replayed from its log to settle a dispute with one answer.

Primal Echo UNREAL ENGINE · C++ · BLUEPRINT

An ecosystem is not a list of enemies. Every creature had to want something, and the player had to be able to read what it wanted.

Engine
Unreal Engine, C++ and Blueprint: Behavior Trees, the Environment Query System, navigation, physics
Game
Primal Echo, a retro-inspired Metroidvania that fuses survival with 3D action-platforming inside an alien megastructure that harbors an ancient ecosystem
Studio
Feral Flame Studios, formerly Nirah Studios AB, Sweden; August 2021 to August 2023
My role
Senior game engineer and tech lead: the whole creature behaviour system, the traversal, and the entire inventory
Team
Internal and external contributors I coordinated, on an average $250K quarterly budget
Result
Prototyping and iteration helped secure $600K+ in investment

The problem

A survival Metroidvania lives or dies on its animals. In Primal Echo you are one more creature in an ecosystem that was there before you arrived: grazers move in herds, stalkers hunt in packs, everything gets hungry, and everything runs when it should. Nothing spawns to fight you. The brief was a simulation deep enough that the food web keeps working with the player nowhere near it, and readable enough that a player can tell from a creature’s posture whether they are about to be ignored, stalked, or charged. Those two goals pull against each other, and holding both is why this dossier is the longest on the page. All of it is Unreal C++ with Blueprint on top for tuning.

One brain for every animal

The first decision was that there would be one behaviour tree, not one per species. A grazer and a stalker are the same machine with different data: what they sense, what they need, what they eat, and what moves they own. The tree has five branches in strict priority (flee, fight, hunt or forage, herd or pack duties, idle), and a drive selector in front of it scores every need against the blackboard each beat and picks the branch. The tree never asks where; that question always goes to EQS, which scores candidate points for food, flanks, escape and cover, and hands one to locomotion. Keeping the three questions apart (what do I want, where do I do it, how do I get there) is what let the same code run a timid herbivore and an apex predator without a special case for either. Fig 5.1

Detection

Sight is two cones, not one. The far cone is narrow and builds awareness slowly; the near cone is wide and builds it fast, so a creature that walks past you close will notice you even if it never looked straight at you. Hearing is a radius fed by the same noise events the rest of the game emits, so a rock you throw is heard by everything in range, not just the thing you aimed at. Scent rides the wind direction, which is why approaching from downwind is a real tactic. All three feed one awareness accumulator on the blackboard that decays when nothing feeds it. The thresholds are where readability lives: at thirty the ears go up and the head turns, at sixty the animal stops and stares, at ninety it commits, to a hunt or to a flight depending on what it is. A player who watches learns the ladder in minutes, and after that every encounter is fair. Fig 5.2

Hunger, and looking for food

Needs are numbers that rise on their own and fall when the creature acts on them. Hunger is the one that runs the ecosystem, because it is the one that makes a creature take risks. Below the first threshold, foraging is not even in the tree. Past it, foraging competes with everything else. Past the second, it outranks fear: a starving grazer will graze next to a stalker, and the stalker will eat. That is not a bug I tuned out; it is the reason the world has carcasses in it, and carcasses are what the scavengers, and a hungry player, are looking for. Finding food is an EQS query: every known food spot is scored by distance, by how safe it is (the scent and sight of anything that eats you counts against it), and by how many others are already eating there. Fig 5.3

SKETCH · C++ · DRIVE SELECTORreduced to the rule that matters
// Every need is scored against the world, every beat. The winner picks the branch. The stick bonus stops flip-flopping.
EDrive UCreatureBrain::SelectDrive(const FBlackboardView& bb)
{
    float best = -1.f; EDrive pick = EDrive::Idle;
    for (const FNeed& n : Needs)
    {
        float score = n.Level * n.Weight;                            // hunger rises with time; fear rises with awareness
        if (n.Drive == EDrive::Flee)   score *= bb.ThreatNearby ? 1.f : 0.f;
        if (n.Drive == EDrive::Forage) score *= bb.KnowsFood    ? 1.f : .4f;   // still worth roaming for
        if (n.Drive == Current)        score += StickBonus;          // stay the course unless clearly beaten
        if (score > best) { best = score; pick = n.Drive; }
    }
    return pick;   // a starving grazer ends up grazing next to a stalker: fear lost, on purpose
}

Prey and predator, from one table

Who hunts whom is not written into any creature. There is one diet table: each species lists what it eats, and that is all anyone authors. What a species fears is derived at load by reversing every arrow, so a grazer is afraid of stalkers because stalkers eat grazers, never because someone remembered to say so. The player is a row in the same table. Adding a species to the world is adding one row, and the whole web, hunts, flights and alarms included, rearranges itself around it. Fig 5.4

The herd

Grazers move as a herd, and the herd is three rules per member per tick: keep your spacing, match the herd’s heading, drift toward its centre. That is enough for a crowd to walk like animals instead of a formation, and it costs almost nothing. What makes it a herd rather than a flock is the shared blackboard. One member that sees a stalker writes the alarm for all of them, so the whole group turns and runs as a unit, away from the threat, before most of them have seen anything. The spacing rule keeps them from piling up on the way; the cohesion rule keeps the group together; and the ones that fall behind are exactly the ones a pack, or a player, ends up picking off. I never wrote a “straggler” system. It emerged. Fig 5.5

The pack

Stalkers hunt together, and a pack that just runs at the prey in a clump is a herd with teeth. The pack has a leader, and the leader assigns roles. It takes the driver role itself: stay behind the prey and keep it running. Everyone else gets a point on a ring around the prey, chosen by EQS. A ring point scores well if a member can reach it, if it is outside the prey’s sight cone, and if nobody has already claimed that sector of the ring. Once a sector is claimed it is removed from the query, so the second flanker is pushed to the other side without anyone writing “go to the other side”. When the prey turns into a flank, the nearest member commits to the attack and the rest re-run the same query from where they are. It is one question, asked again, and it produces encirclement, cut-offs and relays that look planned. Fig 5.6

SKETCH · C++ · PACK ROLESreduced to the rule that matters
// The leader takes the pressure. Everyone else takes the best free point on the ring, in order of who can reach it.
void APackLeader::AssignRoles(const TArray<ACreature*>& pack, const ACreature* prey)
{
    Roles.Reset();
    Roles.Add(this, ERole::Driver);                                        // behind the prey; keeps it running
    TArray<FRingPoint> ring = RunEQS(FlankQuery, prey);                    // scored: reachable, out of its sight, sector free
    for (ACreature* m : SortedByReach(pack, ring))
    {
        if (m == this || ring.IsEmpty()) continue;
        FRingPoint p = ring.Pop();                                         // the best free point
        ring.RemoveAll([&](const FRingPoint& q){ return SameSector(q, p); });  // a claimed sector is gone
        Roles.Add(m, ERole::Flank);
        m->Blackboard->SetVector(TEXT("FlankPoint"), p.Location);
    }
}
// When the prey turns into a flank, the nearest member commits and the rest re-run this from where they stand.
// It is one query, re-asked. The encirclement is what is left after the claimed sectors are removed.

Fight, and knowing when to run

Combat is where the simulation has to become legible, because this is where the player gets hurt. An attack pattern is data: an ordered list of moves, each with a range it is valid at and a telegraph the player can read before it lands. A stalker circles, telegraphs, lunges, bites, backs off, and repeats; an apex has a longer list with a swipe in it. The engage branch walks the list, picking the next move whose range matches where the prey is now, so the same pattern reads differently against a target that stands its ground and one that keeps backing away. The hunt itself is physical: a pounce is a real launch with momentum, a bite is an overlap test during the animation’s active window, and a creature that gets hit is knocked by the force that hit it. Every beat, the creature runs a fight-or-flight check, its health and aggression and the pack advantage against the threat in front of it, and if courage goes below zero the engage branch ends and flee takes over. Flee is an EQS query too: away from the threat, toward cover, the herd, or the den, whichever the species values. Lone stalkers give up fights that packs would win, and it is the same code. Fig 5.7

Traversal

The megastructure is vertical, so the player climbs, wall-jumps, grabs ledges and mantles over them, and none of that geometry is hand-tagged. A mantle is three traces in a fixed order: forward from the chest to find a wall, down from a point above and ahead of it to find the top, and up from the top to make sure there is room to stand. Any geometry that passes all three is climbable; a flag on the surface can say no, which is how the level designers keep you off things you are not meant to reach yet. The height between the wall hit and the top picks the animation: a step, a vault, or a full pull-up. Wall jumps use the contact normal: the launch is the normal plus lift, with a short input window after contact so a late press still jumps. Climbing and wall-jumping share the same probe code, which is what made the parkour feel consistent across rooms built by different people. Fig 5.8

SKETCH · C++ · MANTLE PROBESreduced to the rule that matters
// Three traces, in order. Any geometry that passes is climbable; a surface flag can only say no.
bool UTraversal::TryMantle(FMantleHit& out)
{
    FHitResult wall, top;
    if (!Trace(Chest(), Chest() + Forward() * Reach, wall))       return false;  // 1 · is there a wall?
    if (!wall.Surface->bClimbable)                                 return false;  //     permission is data
    FVector above = wall.ImpactPoint + Forward() * StepIn + FVector::UpVector * MaxLedge;
    if (!Trace(above, above - FVector::UpVector * MaxLedge, top))  return false;  // 2 · where is the top?
    if (!Clear(top.ImpactPoint, CapsuleHeight))                    return false;  // 3 · room to stand?
    out = { top.ImpactPoint, wall.ImpactNormal, top.ImpactPoint.Z - wall.ImpactPoint.Z };
    return true;   // the height picks the animation: a step, a vault, or a full pull-up
}

The inventory, all of it

The inventory is the one system in the game that everything else touches: pickups, crafting, equipment, the corpse you just made, the door that wants a key. So it had to be boring in the right way. An item definition is a data asset (icon, stack size, weight, tags, what “use” does). An item instance is a pointer to one plus a count and a durability, cheap enough to have thousands. A container is a list of slots with rules about capacity and which tags it accepts; the player’s pack, a chest, a corpse and the creature’s own stomach are all containers. Equipment is a container whose slots are named sockets. Adding to a container is one call with a strict order: top up stacks you already carry, fill empty slots, and hand back whatever did not fit so the caller can drop it into the world as a physics item. Nothing is ever silently lost. On the world side there is one interaction interface (focus, prompt, interact) shared by items, doors, levers and carcasses, and the UI reads containers through change events; it never polls. // Merge into stacks you already have, then fill empty slots, then hand back what did not fit. Never lose an item.
int32 UContainer::TryAdd(const UItemDef* def, int32 count)
{
    if (!Allows(def)) return count;                                     // wrong container for this tag
    for (FSlot& s : Slots)                                              // 1 · top up matching stacks
        if (s.Def == def && s.Count < def->StackMax)
        { int32 n = FMath::Min(count, def->StackMax - s.Count); s.Count += n; count -= n; if (!count) break; }
    for (FSlot& s : Slots)                                              // 2 · then empty slots
        if (!s.Def && count) { s.Def = def; s.Count = FMath::Min(count, def->StackMax); count -= s.Count; }
    OnChanged.Broadcast();                                              // the UI listens; it never polls
    return count;                                                       // 3 · the remainder: the caller drops it as a physics item
}

Holographic pipeline UNITY · C++ · SWAVE HXR

Why points

A holographic display does not rasterize triangles. It reconstructs a light field, and the natural input for that computation is a set of point emitters in space, each contributing a wave. Feed it a mesh and you have nothing. Feed it the right points and you get an object hanging in the air. The job was turning what Unity has, shaded surfaces, into what the display wants, well-distributed points, fast enough to be live. Fig 6.1

Slice, keep the shell, sample

The algorithm slices any mesh with a stack of planes, keeps only the outer shell of each slice, and discards the interior. Light inside an object is wasted computation and, worse, visible noise. Points are then sampled on the shell with density proportional to area, so a big flat face and a small detailed one both look solid. Fig 6.2

Randomize on purpose

The samples are jittered. A regular sampling grid lines up with the display’s own pixel structure and produces moiré and ghost patterns in the reconstruction. Randomized positions have no repeating structure to interfere with, and re-randomizing per frame turns any residual artifact into a soft shimmer instead of a fixed pattern. This was the difference between a demo and an object. Fig 6.3

The bridge

The point stream leaves Unity through a C++ pipeline into Swave’s hologram engine, which computes the interference pattern and drives the HXR hardware. Meshes and shaders on the Unity side were optimized for that budget: fewer, better points beat more.

// Shell → points. Area-weighted so big faces stay solid; jittered so no two frames line up.
foreach (var tri in shell.Triangles)
{
    int n = Mathf.CeilToInt(tri.Area * pointsPerUnitArea);
    for (int k = 0; k < n; k++)
    {
        float u = Random.value, v = Random.value;               // uniform on the triangle
        if (u + v > 1f) { u = 1f - u; v = 1f - v; }
        Vector3 p = tri.A + u * (tri.B - tri.A) + v * (tri.C - tri.A);
        p += tri.Normal * Random.Range(-jitter, jitter);           // break the grid, on purpose
        stream.Write(p, tri.Colour);                               // off to the C++ bridge, then the hologram engine
    }
}

The loop

A lesson is a short drill: tap the matching pairs, say the word, pick the right image. Drills pay in coins. Coins build the city. New districts open new vocabulary, which means new drills. The whole game is that loop, gated so you cannot buy your way past words you have not learned. Fig 7.1

An economy that teaches

The design problem with a learning game is that the fun part (building) and the useful part (drilling) pull in opposite directions. The economy is the bridge. Lessons are the only reliable source of coins, buildings are the sink, and the gate to each new district checks level, wallet and mastery of the current words. Streaks and challenges add a little income so a bad day does not stall the city, but they never replace lessons. Fig 7.2
